# Month 9: The Adaptable Core

**Subject:** Month 9: The Adaptable Core

Hi [name],

Most advice about change is useless because it treats adaptability as a
personality trait. You're either flexible or you're not.

That's wrong, and it's a convenient kind of wrong. It lets people off the hook.

Adaptability behaves like a muscle. It responds to load. It strengthens with
deliberate exposure and it atrophies without it. Which means the people who
handle change well aren't built differently, they've just been training
something most of us leave alone.

This month we train it on purpose.

---

## What Actually Separates People Who Handle Change

The research finding worth knowing is counterintuitive. People who adapt well
under pressure aren't the most flexible people. They're the most anchored.

Rock solid on who they are and what matters. Completely loose on the route.

Fixed core, flexible path.

The people who struggle have it inverted. They're rigid about the method and
vague about the point. So when the method stops working, and it always
eventually stops working, there's nothing underneath to navigate by. Every
change feels like a threat to identity, because it is.

This is why the first eight months matter here. You've done the work on style,
values and strengths. That's the core. This month is about how loose you can
afford to be with everything else, precisely because that core is solid.

---

## Three Kinds of Adaptation

Most people only do the first one.

**Reactive adaptation.** Something changes and you respond well. This is the
baseline and it's genuinely valuable, but it's always on the back foot.

**Proactive adaptation.** You see the shift coming and you've moved before it
arrives. This requires paying attention to weak signals rather than waiting for
the obvious ones.

**Innovative adaptation.** You create the change yourself, deliberately, to
gain the advantage. This is rare and it's where the real leverage sits.

Be honest about which one you actually operate at. Most high performers think
they're proactive and are, on inspection, quite good at reactive.

---

## The Pike Syndrome

There's an old experiment with a pike in a tank, separated from its prey by a
glass divider. The pike attacks, hits the glass, attacks again, hits the glass.
Eventually it stops trying.

Then the divider is removed, and the pike starves surrounded by food.

I bring this up every year with athletes and it lands every time, because
everyone has one. A limit they're still working around that stopped existing a
while ago. A conversation they don't have because of how it went in 2019. A
route they don't take because it was blocked once.

The uncomfortable question this month is which glass wall you're still
respecting.

---

You'll baseline where your adaptability actually sits, then look at how you

---

In a world where the ground moves this often, adaptability isn't another skill
on the pile. It's the one that keeps every other skill relevant.

Everything you've built over eight months assumes conditions. Change the
conditions and the value of it depends entirely on whether you can carry it
across.

That's what this month is for.

# Month 10: The Honest Review

**Subject:** Month 10: The Honest Review

Hi [name],

Nine months of writing behind you. This month, the material is your own.

Reflection has a branding problem. It sounds soft, and most people's version of
it is soft, which doesn't help. "That went well." "Could've been better." Then
straight on to the next thing.

Done properly it's the highest leverage habit in the whole programme, and it's
the one most consistently skipped.

---

## Order of Operations

There's one rule that changes reflection from journalling into something
useful.

Start with what happened. Then bring the emotion to it.

Not the other way round.

Do it in reverse, which is what most people do naturally, and the emotion
selects the facts. You feel bad about the meeting, so you remember the moment
you stumbled and forget the eight minutes that went fine. The reflection then
confirms the feeling rather than testing it, and you've spent twenty minutes
reinforcing a distortion.

Facts first is harder and slower. It's also the difference between learning
something and rehearsing something.

---

## Four Levels

Reflection operates at four depths, and most people stop at the first.

**Descriptive.** What actually happened. Just the sequence.

**Analytical.** Why it happened that way. What was driving it.

**Evaluative.** What worked and what didn't, judged against what you were
actually trying to do.

**Future-focused.** What you'll do differently, specifically enough to act on.

Level one alone is a diary. Levels one to three are where most reflection
stops, and it feels productive because it's uncomfortable. But without level
four nothing changes, and discomfort gets mistaken for progress.

---

## The Honesty Problem

The most valuable reflection is honest about your own part. Not self-critical,
which is a different thing and mostly useless, but clear.

Self-criticism is actually a way of avoiding the work. It feels like
accountability while skipping the specifics. "I was rubbish in that meeting" is
easier than "I interrupted twice because I'd already decided the answer before
I walked in."

The second one is uncomfortable in a way that's actionable. The first is just
uncomfortable.

Aim for the version a good coach would give you. Direct about what happened,
uninterested in whether you feel bad about it.

---

more obvious to you now than they were at the time.

---

## Why This Work Matters

Reflection changes how you process what happens next. It primes you to notice
things you'd otherwise walk past, which means the value compounds rather than
staying flat.

We don't learn from experience. We learn from reflecting on experience. Two
people can have identical years and come out of them with wildly different
amounts of usable knowledge, and this is the entire difference.

# Month 11: Behaviour, Not Outcome

**Subject:** Month 11: Behaviour, Not Outcome

Hi [name],

Goals without systems are wishes. Systems without direction are meaningless
motion.

Nearly every goal-setting method obsesses over the target and ignores the
archer. It's all outcome and no behaviour, which is why so many well-set goals
quietly die in February.

Perhaps a better term for the whole thing would be behaviour-setting.

---

## Three Levels, Not One

Most people set goals at one level and wonder why nothing moves.

**Destination.** Where you're going. Far enough out to matter, close enough to
feel real. Three months is usually about right for the first one.

**Milestones.** The markers in service of the destination. Clear, measurable,
challenging but reachable. These exist to give you evidence of progress, which
is the thing that keeps drive alive.

**Systems.** The daily and weekly non-negotiables that make progress
inevitable. This is where almost all of the actual work lives, and it's the
level people skip because it's the least exciting to write down.

A destination with no systems is a wish. Systems with no destination is a
treadmill. You need the whole stack.

---

## Direction of Travel

Before any of that, there's a prior question, and it isn't about metrics.

Who are you becoming through this pursuit?

Identity is the part that sustains a goal when motivation goes. A goal attached
to an outcome dies the moment the outcome looks unlikely. A goal attached to who
you're becoming survives it, because the pursuit itself is the point rather
than the prize at the end.

This is also the filter that catches goals that were never really yours. Plenty
of ambitions are inherited, from a parent, an industry, a version of you from
eight years ago. They're worth catching now rather than in month three of
chasing one.

---

## Direction Over Destination

One more thing worth holding lightly.

Being clear about your direction of travel matters more than being precise
about the endpoint. When you're genuinely moving the right way, opportunities
show up that you couldn't have planned for and wouldn't have written down.

Over-specify the destination and you'll walk past them, because they weren't on
the list.

---

The heaviest week in the chapter, and the one to protect time for. Identity

read like it was written by someone else. Both are useful, and the second kind
is more useful.

---

## Why This Work Matters

The real return on a goal isn't the goal. It's who you become chasing it, and
that's not a consolation prize, it's the actual mechanism.

Understand that and setbacks turn into data. The target stops being a verdict
on you and starts being a direction. You get to keep the compounding even in
the years the outcome doesn't land.

# Month 12: Seasons, Not Balance

**Subject:** Month 12: Seasons, Not Balance

Hi [name],

Last one.

We've been sold a lie about work-life balance. Not because balance is a bad
idea, but because the daily version of it is unachievable, and chasing an
unachievable thing produces guilt rather than change.

Wellbeing isn't a steady state you maintain. It's a dynamic system you
orchestrate.

The answer isn't balance. It's seasons.

---

## The Problem With High Performers

There's a particular trap for people who are good at this.

The drive that creates the success becomes the thing that dismantles it. The
more capable you get, the more arrives. The better your results, the higher the
expectations climb. And because you can absorb it, you do, right up until you
can't.

Nobody notices the slide while it's happening. The baseline drops slowly enough
to look like normal.

Without a deliberate approach to sustainability, excellence eats itself. And the
traditional work-life balance advice actively makes it worse, because it frames
recovery as time taken away from performance rather than as part of it.

---

## How Seasons Actually Work

Anyone who's worked in sport knows this instinctively and then fails to apply
it to their own working life.

No athlete trains at peak intensity year round. They periodise. Blocks of heavy
load, blocks of deliberate recovery, and the recovery is where the adaptation
actually happens. Train through it and you don't get fitter, you get injured.

Same principle, and almost nobody runs their working life this way.

The three pieces:

**Energy architecture.** How you spend, recover and renew. Where your depletion
points actually are rather than where you assume they are.

**Rhythmic living.** Patterns that hold both intensity and restoration, at the
daily, weekly and quarterly scale.

**Integration.** Wellbeing built into the performance system rather than bolted
on as a reward for surviving.

---

## What Happens When You Get It Right

Your baseline rises.

That's the whole return, and it's larger than it sounds. Your worst days start
landing around where your average days used to be. Your best days reach heights
that simply aren't available when you're running depleted, because the ceiling
was never about effort.

Sustainable excellence isn't about longevity as a consolation for lower
performance. It's the thing that unlocks the higher performance.

Twelve months of building capability, and this is the chapter that decides
whether any of it holds.

The foundation is solid. You know how you operate, you have the tools for when
pressure rises, your environment is built for it and you've got direction worth
travelling in. All of that is worth nothing if you can't sustain the conditions
that make it work.

Worth saying plainly at the end. This isn't a finish line, it's an infinity
loop. The chapters are built to be returned to, and they read differently from
where you're standing now than they did the first time round.

You've got the full system. Twelve months of evidence that you use it.

Speak soon,
